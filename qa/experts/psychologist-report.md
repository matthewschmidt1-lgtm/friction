# Friction: review by Dr. Rachel Okafor (organizational psychology / Conscious Leadership)

Method note: I wrote the five answer sets from the visible question text. My first extraction of the file printed the opener and a few early `sig` weights before I had finished answering, so I cannot claim a perfectly blind run. The answers are in character and none were tuned to the weights. Replay file: `qa/experts/psychologist-answers.json` (O1 to O5).

## Summary verdict

Friction is a competent structural diagnostic: clean writing, honest about uncertainty, with experiments a real team could run. It is not yet safe or accurate for the problems my field sees most. Conflict avoidance, blame, fear in the leader, and grief are nearly invisible to it, and in three of my five cases the candour question was never asked at all. The tool also shows "Candour = High" as an observation when it never measured it. Where the leader is the problem, the questions let the leader place the problem elsewhere, so the read tends to confirm the leader's story. It keeps people above the line only when they already arrive there.

Mean usefulness across my five organizations: **2.2 / 5**.

## The five test organizations

### O1. Artificial harmony: 40-person consultancy, CEO answering
**Situation.** The partners are warm, thank each other in every meeting, and agree to everything. Nothing is ever decided, because nobody will be the one who says no. Projects slip and nobody is told so. The CEO sees a "follow-through problem" and a team she likes.

**How the CEO answered in character.** Opener: "We agree on things that then don't happen." Leaders "mostly similar". Decisions stall because "we keep revisiting". Decisions wait on "Agreement between leaders". Execution "sometimes", and the reasons are follow-through and no clear owner. Team safety "usually safe".

**Read.** "Agreed priorities don't reliably happen" at Emerging, with "Leadership isn't aligned" and "Decision rights" as rivals. Experiment: three leaders each write the top three priorities, then publish one list. Blind spot: "commitments are aspirations".

**Judgment.**
- This is a polished description of the symptom with the cause missing.
- Candour and conflict avoidance were never asked. `trust` was not selected at all, so a 10-question interview about a team that cannot disagree contained no question about disagreement.
- Under "What supports this" the page lists "Commitments lapse without consequence". She never said that. She picked the word "Follow-through". The `obs` text is stronger than the answer (see finding F3).
- The experiment is sound for a harmonious team. "Do not leave until it is one list" is a good instruction here.
- The "Expected vs observed" table says **Candour = High**. Candour was never measured (finding F1).

Where it helped: "What did we commit to last quarter that didn't happen, and what did we do about it?" is a good question. Where it failed: it never suggests that the CEO's own niceness may be part of the system. **Usefulness 2.**

### O2. Founder whose fear drives control: 60-person SaaS, founder answering
**Situation.** The founder nearly lost the company in 2022. Since then he reverses managers' calls "only when it matters", rewrites decks, and wants to be cc'd on everything. Good people are leaving. He experiences himself as approachable and exhausted.

**Answers.** Opener: "Too many decisions come to me." Decisions stall because "Nobody clearly owns it". Authority "usually" sufficient. Managers' decisions "get reversed". Safe to disagree "usually"; the last problem raised "was acted on". Limits to action: "Lack of capability". Worse if he "keeps solving the symptom".

**Read.** "Decisions get stuck", rooted in unclear decision rights, with "centralized" as the runner-up ("We changed our mind: earlier it pointed to Decision authority is more centralized"). Gap: Enablement. Blind spot: "normalised management intervention". Experiment: write decision rights for three stalled decisions. What not to do: "Don't fix this by adding another approval layer. Your answers suggest the problem is not insufficient control."

**Judgment.**
- This is the worst failure of the five. The founder reversed decisions and the tool concluded nobody owns them.
- His escape hatch was the option "Nobody clearly owns it". It is plausible, flattering, and structural.
- The page then reassures him: "the problem is not insufficient control."
- A decision-rights chart written by the founder, with the founder as final approver, is the likely outcome.
- The last "ONE QUESTION" ("...because we don't trust the organization to make them") is the only line that points at him, and it is phrased as "we".
- The blind spot ("organization may have normalised management intervention") names what the organization does, not what he does.
- No sentence anywhere says: your answers include that you reverse managers' calls, and that is the strongest single piece of evidence you gave.

**Usefulness 3.** The structural work is not wrong, but this read delays the real conversation.

### O3. Organization in grief and crisis: 120-person manufacturer, 4 months after the COO's sudden death and a 15% layoff
**Situation.** The COO was the glue and the informal decider. The layoff followed three weeks later. Survivors are exhausted, guilty, and quiet. Nobody says what they fear. The CEO is also grieving and cannot name it.

**Answers.** Opener: "Effort is high but results aren't." No clarity on what to stop. Agreed things happen "sometimes", with "no clear owner" and "lack of capacity". Best people "rarely" on high-value work. Authority "sometimes". Inversion: avoid the difficult conversation, and do nothing.

**Read.** "Too many priorities, and no clear sense of what to stop." Experiment: a stop-doing list. The tool noticed that the real move is decision rights ("Why this experiment... but the highest-value move is to act on 'Decision rights are unclear'") and then showed the decision-rights experiment. Blind spot: "normalised overload as a sign of ambition".

**Judgment.**
- This is wrong in its emotional frame and, in places, harmful.
- Calling an organization in mourning one that "normalised overload as a sign of ambition" is glib, close to cruel, and wrong.
- "Stopping something feels like failure" is a nice insight, but nowhere on the page does the word loss, grief, or fear appear.
- "Announce the stop-doing list as a decision" is a reasonable work item, but delivered cold it adds a second round of loss to a team that has had one.
- It rated me "Candour = High" when candour had not been asked and the honest truth is the opposite.
- Partial credit: it did locate the missing role ("nobody clearly owns it") that follows from the COO's death, and that is real.
- No safety net exists for a leader who is in pain. Nothing says: this tool is the wrong instrument for the first thing you need.

**Usefulness 1.**

### O4. Blame and gossip: 200-person logistics firm, CEO answering
**Situation.** Sales says ops fails; ops says IT never delivers; IT says Sales promises things that don't exist. The CEO hears each version in private and sighs. He believes the VPs "don't agree about what matters". Hallway gossip runs the information system.

**Answers.** Opener: "Leaders don't agree on what matters." Top-three lists "quite different". Decisions stall because "Leaders disagree". Authority "sometimes". Waiting on "Agreement between leaders". Inversion: avoid the difficult conversation, and continue rewarding the current behavior.

**Read.** "Leadership isn't aligned on what matters most" at Strong. Blind spot: "Your leadership team may have learned to treat polite agreement as alignment." Experiment: each leader writes their top three, compare in one room, and "do not leave until it is one list".

**Judgment.**
- The diagnosis is right at the surface and entirely below the line. It frames "leaders don't agree" as a strategy-selection problem.
- No question was asked about blame, gossip, or safety. `trust` never reached the CEO, although "Avoid the difficult conversation" was ticked.
- The blind spot template is for the wrong disease: "polite agreement" in a team that is not polite.
- The experiment is risky as written. Showing everyone the lists and demanding one list in the room, with no ground rules, hands a blaming team new ammunition. It needs a facilitator and a no-fault frame.
- Positive: "If our leadership team's top-three lists differ, whose list is the organization actually executing?" is a good question, and it includes the CEO.
- The tool never tells the CEO that he is the one hearing each leader's version privately. That is his part.

**Usefulness 2.**

### O5. Healthy, high-candour team: 35-person design firm
**Situation.** Disagreement is normal and brief, nobody punishes anyone for bad news, and the founders regularly say "I was wrong". The only friction is too many good ideas.

**Answers.** Opener: "Too many priorities" (the closest honest choice). Every other answer is on the healthy side; safety was "Very safe".

**Read.** "Too many priorities, and no clear sense of what to stop" at Emerging, with candid "What cuts against it". Experiment: stop the bottom fifth, then watch a month.

**Judgment.**
- This is the best output of the five and a mild false positive.
- The opener forces a problem. There is no "nothing is really in the way" option, so LOW_FRICTION could never fire, because the opener is a mandatory selection that builds a hypothesis.
- The blind spot "normalised overload as a sign of ambition" is a plausible insight for a team that is overloaded, and presumptuous for one that says it is not.
- It did not ask about the leaders' own state or about appreciation and play, so there is nothing useful for a healthy team to learn.
- It is correct that the experiment is cheap and low risk.

**Usefulness 3.**

**Mean: (2 + 3 + 1 + 2 + 3) / 5 = 2.2.**

## Question bias findings

Severity high / medium / low.

**F1. "Candour" shown as observed when it was never asked. HIGH.**
Location: `PROFILE_ROWS`, `{ k: 'Candour' ... observe: p => p.trust >= .65 ? 'Low' : p.trust >= .45 ? 'Mixed' : 'High' }`.
Problem: the default is "High", so an unmeasured dimension is presented as a measured good one. In O1, O2 and O3 the page said "Candour = High". In O3 that is plainly false.
Fix: return "Not asked" (shown as a grey dash, with "we did not ask") when no `trust`-family question was answered. Same for any row driven only by prior.

**F2. `trust` is under-asked, and self-report is accepted at face value. HIGH.**
Location: question `trust` ("When someone sees a problem or disagrees with a decision, how safe is it to say so directly?") and the selection logic (`trust` prior .20 in `NETWORK`, `gate` on `trust_last`).
Problem: in O1, O3 and O4 the question was never reached. When it is reached, the person answering is usually the person whose reaction determines the answer. "Very safe" scores `trust: -1.7`, nearly closing the door, and leaders reliably give this answer.
Fix: always ask one candour question in the first six, regardless of VOI, and rewrite it so that the leader looks at behaviour, not climate:
> "Think of the last time someone told you something you didn't want to hear. What did you do in the next ten seconds?"
> Options: "Thanked them and asked more" / "Listened, then explained why I saw it differently" / "Got visibly tense or went quiet" / "Moved on to something else" / "I can't remember anyone telling me something like that."
The last option should raise the trust estimate. Reduce the `-1.7` on "Very safe" to about `-0.7`, and let a gap between this answer and the team picture lift the blind spot.

**F3. Evidence text stronger than the answer. HIGH.**
Location: `execution_why` "Follow-through" `obs: 'Commitments lapse without consequence'`; `focus`/`business_uncertainty` "What to stop" `obs: 'Nothing is formally stopped'`; `analysis` options.
Problem: the page lists these under "What supports this" as if the respondent said them. In O1 she chose a single word and the page told her she had said commitments lapse without consequence. That undermines the claim to show evidence honestly.
Fix: render the respondent's own words ("You said: 'Follow-through'") and keep inferences in the "our inference" lines, which the tool already separates well.

**F4. Options let the respondent place the problem elsewhere; "it's me" is almost never available. HIGH.**
- `decisions`: "Nobody clearly owns it" (`decision_rights: 1.6`) is the default exit for a controlling founder. Add "I find it hard to let go of it" and "I decide after others have discussed it".
- `comeback`: "Lack of confidence in the person" (`capability: 1.5`) charges the other person. Rewrite as "I am not confident the person will get it right" so the leader owns the judgment. Add "I want to be the one who decides" and "I get nervous when it is out of my hands".
- `given`: all five options describe the decider's behaviour. Add "They decide, and I step in".
- `people_limits`: "Leadership behavior" is the only option that looks at the leader, last in the list, and is weighted only `trust: .9, centralized: .8`. Rewrite as "How I and the other leaders respond when something goes wrong" and move it to the top. This is the Conscious Leadership move: begin where you are.
- `overrule` "It gets reversed" gives `centralized: 1.5`, which is good, but the `comeback`/`decisions` answers then overwrite it. When a respondent reports reversals, the posterior on `centralized` must not fall below a floor, and the read should say so ("You said you reverse managers' decisions").

**F5. Opener is externalised and has no "nothing" option. MEDIUM-HIGH.**
Seven of eight openers describe "we", "they", "people". Only the first mentions the respondent, and as a victim ("Too many decisions come to me"). There is no "I'm not sure", "something is wrong that I can't name", or "nothing major".
Fix: add "I'm tired, and I can't tell if it's the business or me" (no direct weight, forces the emotional probe in F6), "Nothing major is in the way" (routes to LOW_FRICTION), and rewrite "Too many decisions come to me" as "Too many decisions end up with me."

**F6. `direction` measures what the CEO thinks the team would say. MEDIUM-HIGH.**
"How similar would their answers be?" with "Almost identical" at `direction: -1.6` invites a socially desirable and uninformed guess. Harmonious teams answer "mostly similar". The tool already has a good behavioural test in the blind spot ("ask each leader separately"), but the question that precedes it cannot detect harmony.
Fix: add a companion item:
> "When did two of your leaders last disagree openly, in front of the team, about direction?"
> Options: "This month" / "This quarter" / "I can't remember one" / "Never".
> "Can't remember"/"Never" should raise a new `conflict_avoidance` hypothesis (see breadth gaps), not lower `direction`.

**F7. `waiting` "Agreement between leaders" maps to `direction`. MEDIUM.**
In O1 it came from politeness, not disagreement. The same answer can mean "we disagree" or "we avoid saying no". Add the option "Nobody wants to be the one who says no" and map it to `conflict_avoidance`.

**F8. Inversion question is cognitively strong but structurally limited. MEDIUM.**
The options are all management actions. None names the leader's emotional behaviours: "Stay busy so I don't have to feel it", "Protect my own position", "Keep it comfortable", "Take it personally". "Do nothing" carries no weights, and its `force` text claims "Nothing about the current pattern requires anyone to act, so it persists" for any respondent who clicks it, even for reasons of exhaustion.
Fix: add "Take the criticism personally and defend myself" and "Keep things comfortable instead of saying what I see", and let "Do nothing" carry `execution: .3`.

**F9. Respondent as the problem: the engine cannot ask it. MEDIUM.**
Nothing in the pool is about the person answering: their state, their fear, their last behaviour. Add a self-position block (see breadth gaps). "Be honest about which ones are already happening" on the inversion screen is a good start and should be applied elsewhere.

**F10. Size, sector and structure assumptions. MEDIUM.**
- `economics`: "customers, revenue, margin, cash" excludes nonprofits (mission, funders), public sector (statute, budget cycle), family business (family continuity), and professional partnerships (partner economics).
- `direction`: assumes a "leadership team" exists. In a 12-person company the leader is the team. Add a screen at the start: "How many people are on your leadership team?" with "I am the leadership team" as a valid answer that rewrites later phrasing.
- `focus`: "eliminate 20% of your current priorities" assumes a portfolio of discrete priorities.
- `decisions`, `comeback`: board and owner decisions are invisible (e.g., a founder reporting to a board, or a family council).

**F11. Weights and what they favour. MEDIUM.**
- Every negative-weight option (`Almost identical` -1.6, `Definitely` -1.6, `Very safe` -1.7, `They don't come back up` -1.4, `Decisions don't really get stuck` -1.3, `Almost always` -1.5 to -1.6) can near-eliminate a hypothesis on one answer from a person who may be the cause. They should be capped near -0.8 for any item where the respondent is the cause.
- The five structural hypotheses (`decision_rights`, `information`, `execution`, `leverage`, `focus`) get at least five or six upward routes each. The behavioural ones (`trust`, `capability`) get far fewer. `trust` has only three direct routes (`trust`, `trust_last`, `people_limits`), and gating means that `trust_last` is only offered after `trust` fires.
- The combined effect: ties between a structural and a behavioural explanation go to the structural one, which is easier to say and easier to act on. That is consistent with the O2 result.

**F12. Experiments assume a safe room. MEDIUM.**
`direction` step 2 ("Compare the lists in one room. Do not leave until it is one list") can turn into a fight in a blaming team and a charade in a harmonious one. Add a prerequisite line: "If anyone is likely to be blamed for their list, start with the one-to-one version and a facilitator." The `trust` experiment is the best one in the file (the most senior person speaks last, thank the first person, act within the week), and this is the model for the others.

**F13. Blind-spot templates mismatched to the case. MEDIUM.**
The blind spot is selected by the read, not by the answers. O4 got "polite agreement", O3 got "normalised overload as ambition", and O2 got a generic organizational line. Each should either be conditional on a supporting answer, or be turned into a pair of ways it could be wrong.

**F14. Confidence labels in a healthy case. LOW.**
"Emerging pattern" appeared for O5 on one weak signal. For the user, "Emerging pattern" reads as "something is wrong". Fix: if all answers cut against every hypothesis, route to LOW_FRICTION even if the opener names one.

## Breadth gaps

What a skilled organizational therapist expects and the pool cannot see:

1. **Conflict avoidance / artificial harmony** (new hypothesis `conflict_avoidance`). Signs: no open disagreement, agreement then silence, decisions that "wait for agreement". Suggested questions: the F6 item; "In your last three leadership meetings, how many times did someone change their mind because of what another person said?"; "How often does a meeting end with an agreement that sounds firmer than it is?" Blind spot: "Your team may have learned that agreeing is how you show respect." Experiment: one meeting where each person must state a view that is different from the previous speaker's.
2. **Blame and gossip (below the line)** (`blame`). Questions: "When something goes wrong, what is the first question asked?" ("Who?" / "What happened?" / "What did we do to create this?" / "We don't talk about it"); "How often do you hear about a colleague from a third person before you hear it from them?" Experiment: for one month, any complaint about a person must be taken to them first (the gossip commitment), starting with the CEO.
3. **The leader's own state and part** (`leader_fear` or `self`). Fear of losing the company, fear of being exposed, need to be right, need to be needed. Questions: "When the company is at its most stressed, what do you reach for?" ("More data" / "More meetings" / "Doing it myself" / "Calling a friend or coach" / "Waiting it out"); "What do you worry people would say about you if they felt safe?" The tool's mechanism for the leader's part is currently only one line in `people_limits` and the one-question text.
4. **Grief, loss and change fatigue** (`loss`). Questions: "In the last twelve months, has the organization lost someone or something it cared about (a person, a client, a product, a layoff)?" with a follow-up "Has that been talked about openly?" If yes and unprocessed, the result should say plainly that this is not a structure problem first, name it, and suggest a facilitated conversation before any experiment. Also add a "this may not be the right tool" screen.
5. **Say/do gap, integrity, and values** (`integrity`). Commitments made and broken to each other, or to customers; "Do your leaders do what they said they would do, and say so when they can't?" Related to the unclear "follow-through" in `execution_why`.
6. **Power and the CEO's relationship to the board, investors, partners or family** (`governance`). Particularly for family businesses and founder-led firms: succession, unspoken rules about who may be challenged.
7. **Energy and burnout**. "Effort is high but results aren't" gets read as a focus problem. Burnout is a capacity of the people, not a priority issue. A question about sleep, leave taken and sickness absence would change the diagnosis.
8. **Appreciation, play, genius.** No hypothesis can say a team is working under-appreciated or has the right people in the wrong role beyond `talent`. A question such as "When did you last hear a colleague say 'thank you, that mattered'?" is cheap and diagnostic.
9. **Market-originating friction.** Not every pain is internal. The tool assumes the friction is inside the company, and gives no way to say "this is a customer or market problem".

Without items 1 to 4, the tool systematically misses the situations in which a leader most needs a good conversation.

## What the tool does well

- It leads with candour about its own uncertainty ("Still trying to understand", "We changed our mind", "What cuts against it", "Other explanations weighed"). That is the right model for an honest diagnostic.
- Showing no score is a good decision. A number would invite defensiveness.
- The blind-spot framing as a falsifiable test with "If true / If false" is excellent, and I would teach it.
- The `trust` playbook and experiment are the strongest content: "The most senior person speaks last", "Thank the first person who names a real problem, publicly", "Reward the messenger". The counterfactual "Don't fix this with an anonymous survey" is exactly right.
- The "What not to do" counterfactuals are specific and useful.
- Closing questions such as "whose list is the organization actually executing?" and "What did we commit to last quarter that didn't happen, and what did we do about it?" are strong and transferable to a leadership meeting.
- The transparent line "the read is X, but the highest-value move is Y" shows its reasoning, even if the reasoning is sometimes wrong.
- The inversion question is a smart device. "Be honest about which ones are already happening" is the one place the tool addresses the respondent's own behaviour.

## Top five changes, in priority order

1. **Stop displaying unmeasured things as findings (F1, F3).** "Candour = High" where candour was not asked, and `obs` text stronger than the answer, are honesty failures. Show "Not asked", and show the respondent's actual words.
2. **Always ask a behaviour-based candour question and a "your own part" question, and make "it's me" available (F2, F4, F9).** Rewrite `trust` into the ten-second behavioural question. Add "I find it hard to let go" to `decisions`, "I am not confident the person will get it right" to `comeback`, and move "How I and the other leaders respond when something goes wrong" to the top of `people_limits`. Floor `centralized` when the respondent reports reversals, so that a founder who reverses decisions never gets the read "nobody owns it" without the sentence "you said you reverse decisions".
3. **Add three hypotheses: `conflict_avoidance`, `blame`, `loss` (breadth gaps 1, 2, 4).** Give each two questions, a playbook, a blind spot, and an experiment. Add a hard stop for `loss`: the first output is "this is not mainly a structure problem; here is what to do first", before any structural experiment.
4. **Give the tool a way to say "nothing" and "not the right tool" (F5, F14).** Add "Nothing major is in the way" and "I'm tired and can't tell" to the opener, so LOW_FRICTION can fire and so a leader in pain is not given a priority-pruning exercise.
5. **Make blind spots and experiments conditional on the case, with safety prerequisites (F12, F13).** Choose the blind spot from the answers, not only from the read. Add a line to the `direction` experiment: "If anyone is likely to be blamed for their list, use a facilitator and start one-to-one." Replace "we" with "I" in the closing question for `centralized`: "Which decisions am I still making because I don't trust the organization to make them, and what am I afraid will happen if I stop?"
