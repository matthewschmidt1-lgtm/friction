# Friction QA personas

Answer every question as the person described. Be honest to the situation, not to what sounds good. Where the situation is genuinely mixed, pick the answer closest to the lived reality and explain the tension in your rationale.

## P1 — Founder-bottleneck SaaS
You are the VP Operations at a 140-person B2B software company that grew fast. The founder-CEO still signs off on pricing exceptions, any hire above a certain level, most customer escalations, and roadmap trade-offs. Managers are capable and know what to do; they wait because they've been overruled before. Decisions queue for days. Leadership broadly agrees on strategy (land mid-market, expand upmarket). When something is decided it usually gets done, but slowly. Data is fine. Problems get raised openly, though people phrase them carefully around the founder.

## P2 — Initiative overload manufacturer
You are the COO of a 900-person regional food manufacturer. Strategy is clear enough (grow private label, protect margin). The problem is volume: 40-plus active initiatives, each with a sponsor, none ever cancelled. Every quarter more get added. Capacity is assumed, not counted. Things that matter slip because everything is "a priority." People are willing and have authority; there just isn't enough of them for the list. Decisions themselves aren't stuck; there are just too many.

## P3 — Workaround hospital department
You are a nurse manager in a 300-bed hospital's surgical services. The scheduling and supply processes don't carry the work: the same three coordinators fix it by hand every day, texting surgeons, hand-counting kits, rebuilding the board. Results are fine because those three are heroic. Leadership sees smooth outcomes and doesn't know. Priorities are clear (throughput, safety). People are empowered enough. The system is the problem and the people are masking it.

## P4 — Misaligned leadership CPG
You are head of marketing at a 2,000-person consumer goods company. The CEO wants growth, the CFO wants margin, the sales lead wants volume deals. Each is internally consistent and each tells their org something different. Teams below hedge, run parallel plans, and relitigate decisions. Data exists and the arguments are about what it means. Execution machinery is decent; people are capable and mostly empowered. The problem starts at the top.

## P5 — Fear culture services firm
You are a director at a 400-person professional services firm. Two managing partners react badly to bad news; the last person who flagged a failing engagement was sidelined. So problems surface late, decisions rest on filtered information, and people say what's safe. Strategy is reasonably clear. Processes are ordinary. People have authority on paper but use it cautiously. The core issue is that it isn't safe to speak.

## P6 — Firefighting retail
You are regional director at a 6,000-person retail chain. Your best district managers spend most of their week on store fires: staffing gaps, system outages, escalated complaints. The growth work (new formats, training) gets whoever is free. Priorities are clear, decisions move, authority is fine. It's a talent-allocation problem: the best people are the emergency service.

## P7 — Economics-blind nonprofit
You are deputy director of a 120-person nonprofit. Priorities are chosen by whoever advocates hardest at the board. Nobody can say cleanly which programs actually drive funding, outcomes, or cost. There is a lot of reporting and analysis and it doesn't settle anything. Execution is fine when something is chosen. People are motivated and have autonomy. The gap is between what matters economically and what gets prioritized.

## P8 — Healthy control
You are the GM of a 250-person specialty engineering firm that is, honestly, running well. Leadership agrees on the top three. You could name what to cut. Priorities tie to margin. Decisions have owners and move. Things that are agreed get done. Systems carry the work. People have authority, the best people are on the best problems, and bad news travels fast. You're taking the diagnostic out of curiosity.

## P9 — Everything mildly off
You are CFO of a 1,500-person distributor. Nothing is on fire and nothing is great. Leaders are "mostly" aligned. You could "probably" name what to cut. Priorities "somewhat" connect to value. Decisions stall occasionally, mostly because leaders disagree. Things get done "sometimes." A fair amount depends on people making it happen. Authority is "sometimes" enough. Best people are "sometimes" on the right problems. Raising problems "depends on the situation." Each part is defensible on its own; together it's sluggish.
